The migration adds one nullable column, `sales.sales_order_lines.price_source`. Existing lines
get null, which is exactly right: nothing recorded where their price came from, and pretending
otherwise would be inventing history.


WHAT THIS IS

A sales line now says which part and how many, and nothing else:

    POST /api/sales/orders/{id}/lines
    { "partId": "...", "quantity": 10 }

The catalogue names the part and gives its stocking unit. Pricing gives the price — the
customer's list, the right quantity break, their agreed discount. The line records which price
list answered.

That completes what the four contracts were for. A year ago this call took a part id, a SKU, a
description, a unit, a price and a discount, and six of those seven were the caller repeating
something a module already knew.


THE OVERRIDE STAYS
------------------

    { "partId": "...", "quantity": 10, "unitPrice": 18.00 }

A manager knocking a tenner off at the counter happens several times a day, and a system that
cannot express it is a system where somebody edits the database instead. Supplying a price skips
Pricing entirely and the line records no price list — which is the honest answer to "why that
figure?", and a different answer from "the trade list said so".

Supplying only a discount keeps the quoted price and replaces the customer's agreed discount.


WHY PriceSource EXISTS
----------------------

Three weeks after the invoice, somebody asks why a figure was what it was. The two honest answers
are "the trade list said so" and "Miguel overrode it". Both are fine; not knowing which is not.

Re-deriving it later means re-running rules that have since moved — the list may have been
repriced, the promotion may have expired, the customer may have been renegotiated. So the
document records it at the moment it is decided, alongside the SKU and the description, which are
snapshotted for the same reason.

It is the price list's CODE, not its id: a document that says "TRADE" explains itself to a person
reading it, and a document that says a GUID does not.


TWO REFUSALS
------------

    sales.line.no_price                  404, and it names the SKU
    sales.line.price_currency_mismatch   422

`IPriceProvider` returns null when nothing prices a part, rather than an error, because a contract
cannot say anything useful about it — Sales knows the SKU and the order number, so Sales writes
the refusal. Pricing's own /quote endpoint distinguishes the several reasons (no list, expired
agreement, below the smallest pack) when somebody needs to know which.

The currency check is a second copy of one Pricing already does, deliberately. The contract has no
opinion about what currency the caller's document is in, so the caller has to have one — and the
alternative is a line quietly converted at whatever rate somebody configured last year.

WHAT IS LEFT
------------

1. `ChangeSalesOrderLinePricing` still takes a price and a discount and does not touch Pricing.
   That is correct for what it is — an explicit repricing of an existing line — but it does not
   clear PriceSource, so a line quoted from TRADE and then overridden still claims TRADE. Worth
   fixing next; it is a two-line change to the aggregate and a test.

2. Nothing re-quotes an order. A draft raised on Monday and confirmed on Friday keeps Monday's
   prices, which is usually what a customer expects and occasionally is not. A "reprice this
   order" command would be the honest way to offer the alternative.

3. Purchasing still takes its price by hand. Supplier price files are their own problem — they
   arrive as spreadsheets, per supplier, in whatever shape that supplier feels like — and are a
   module rather than a contract call.
